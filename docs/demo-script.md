## 00:00 - 01:00 | NEPHRA

Welcome to NEPHRA, a working digital twin proof of concept for the Happiest Health Digital Twin Challenge. Our focus is a specific problem in maintenance haemodialysis: anticipating a fall in systolic blood pressure over the next thirty minutes. The prototype combines a synthetic historical patient record with a changing stream of medical sensor observations. It updates a patient-specific model, shows a forecast, and lets a reviewer explore a hypothetical scenario. It also prepares a review draft automatically. Every patient and every observation in this demonstration is synthetic. No clinical accuracy, treatment benefit, or regulatory approval is claimed. The goal is to demonstrate a useful and inspectable engineering workflow, then define the evidence needed to test that workflow responsibly in a real clinical setting.

## 01:00 - 02:00 | The problem: a pressure fall during dialysis

During dialysis, fluid is removed from the circulation. Fluid also moves back into the circulation from other compartments. A pressure fall can occur when removal and compensatory processes are not adequately balanced. The clinical situation is more complex than this description: cardiovascular function, medicines, symptoms and treatment context all matter. KDIGO identifies symptomatic pressure decreases or an intradialytic systolic pressure below ninety millimetres of mercury as reasons for reassessment. Staff already monitor patients, but a sequence of observations can be more informative than a single value. Our problem statement therefore focuses on preparing a forward-looking view that brings those observations together. We are not proposing less clinical supervision, and we are not replacing the established protocol of a dialysis unit.

## 02:00 - 03:00 | A narrow outcome makes the twin testable

The challenge asks for a localised health outcome rather than an entire virtual human. We chose an absolute systolic-pressure threshold within a thirty-minute window. That endpoint gives us a clear question, a clear observation horizon, and a reproducible way to compare predictions with future synthetic measurements. It does not capture every clinical definition of intradialytic hypotension. Symptoms, relative pressure changes and intervention requirements are outside this narrow endpoint. The intended user is a nurse or nephrologist reviewing a multi-patient dialysis unit. The intended workflow is observation, interpretation, comparison and documentation. Existing research already predicts dialysis hypotension, so we do not claim that early prediction itself is novel. Our proposed contribution is the inspectable combination of prediction, scenario tradeoffs and preparation of a review draft.

## 03:00 - 04:00 | Two streams, one changing patient state

The first stream is a synthetic historical patient record. It contains a dry weight, baseline pressure, and explicit physiological priors for refilling and pressure sensitivity. It also includes display context such as age, comorbidities and prior episodes. Those priors are supplied for the demonstration; they have not been fitted to actual patient sessions. The second stream contains changing observations. Cuff pressure is available every five minutes, while relative blood volume, pulse and fluid-removal observations appear every minute. Pulse is shown for context but is not a predictor feature in this version. The model fuses historical parameters with the reliable observations available at the selected time. The result is a state that changes as new information arrives, rather than a static score or a decorative patient avatar.

## 04:00 - 05:00 | The implemented architecture

This diagram shows the implemented system. A static server delivers the application, and all patient computation runs inside the browser. Historical records and sensor observations pass through schema checks and quality checks. A temporal gate excludes observations after the current replay time. The state estimator then updates the circulating-volume deficit and estimates current refilling and pressure trend. The forecast and the scenario simulator start from this same state. Their outputs feed the dashboard and a deterministic review draft. A reviewer can record a local review marker and export the draft. There is no path to a dialysis machine, no hospital connection, and no cloud patient database. The evaluation script is separate: it generates additional synthetic sessions and uses future truth only to score predictions, never to inform a current forecast.

## 05:00 - 06:00 | The working dialysis command centre

Here is an actual capture of the working application. The left side lists six synthetic treatment sessions. Each patient has a different historical record and a separately seeded observation stream. The summary distinguishes priority reviews from watch cases, and the selected patient panel shows measured pressure, pulse, relative blood volume and fluid removed. The chart separates observed history from the forward projection. The replay control changes the available observation window for every patient at once. This lets a reviewer compare the same point in different sessions without confusing wall-clock time with treatment time. The orange synthetic-demo label and the research boundary remain visible. The interface is functional: selecting a patient changes the observations and the forecast, rather than merely switching names on a fixed chart.

## 06:00 - 07:00 | Read the forecast, not just the latest number

For the default synthetic patient at minute one hundred and twenty, the latest cuff is approximately ninety-five millimetres of mercury. Looking only at that number would not describe the direction of travel. The model projects a continuing decline, with a central trajectory reaching the reference threshold in roughly fourteen minutes in this deterministic example. The chart makes the distinction visible: a solid observed line, a dashed future line and a sensitivity band. That band is deliberately not labelled as a ninety-five-percent confidence interval. Its width follows a heuristic based on horizon, recent residual error and disagreement between model components. A stable label only means no modelled crossing in this window. It does not certify that a real patient is safe or that routine monitoring can stop.

## 07:00 - 08:00 | A mechanistic model with online correction

The core algorithm is a reduced state-space model. Fluid removal increases an estimated circulating-volume deficit, while refilling decreases it. Relative blood volume supplies a noisy observation of that state. A scalar Kalman update combines the predicted state with the measurement. Recent changes in the blood-volume stream also update the refilling estimate, and a least-squares fit to recent cuff readings estimates the pressure trend. The forward pressure change combines seventy percent mechanistic projection and thirty percent trend correction. These coefficients are transparent demonstration assumptions, not clinically learned parameters. The model is adaptive because sensor information changes its internal state, but it is not a trained neural network. The source code and model card expose every equation and limitation so a reviewer can inspect exactly how the displayed result was produced.

## 08:00 - 09:00 | Compare an isolated hypothetical scenario

The scenario lab starts with the same patient state and the same observation history. The only changed input is a hypothetical fluid-removal rate. In this captured example, the observed stream is zero point eight eight litres per hour and the comparison is zero point six two. The model predicts a different pressure trajectory and calculates how much fluid would be removed over the next thirty minutes. Both results appear together. Nothing about this interaction modifies the historical record, changes the replay stream or sends a command to a device. Selecting Match observed rate returns the scenario to the original input and produces the same projected nadir and the same removed volume. That identity check is useful evidence that the comparison is calculated from shared state rather than composed from unrelated fixed numbers.

## 09:00 - 10:00 | Every scenario exposes a tradeoff

The important part of scenario exploration is the tradeoff. A gentler hypothetical removal rate can yield a higher projected pressure while leaving more fluid unremoved in the same time. The prototype displays that difference in litres alongside the pressure comparison. It does not label a scenario as the best treatment. Real decisions need symptoms, current volume status, dialysis adequacy, medicines, clinician judgement and patient preferences. Those are not captured by this simplified model. A counterfactual curve is therefore a statement about the model's assumptions, not proof of what will happen to a patient after a treatment change. This is also why the interface uses hypothetical language and why every exported review says that no treatment action was sent. The useful research question is whether this comparison helps clinicians reason without encouraging over-reliance.

## 10:00 - 11:00 | When observations fail, the forecast stops

This capture shows the deliberate sensor-dropout test. Recent observations have been marked unreliable. The application does not carry forward a reassuring green forecast or invent replacement measurements. It displays Forecast withheld and explains the missing requirements. The model needs a sufficiently recent reliable stream, a recent cuff value, and at least three good cuff readings in its recent window. Restoring sensor quality allows estimation to resume. The same principle applies to imported sessions that end before the selected replay time: the observations become stale and the forecast is withheld. Automated tests cover unreliable data, stale records and insufficient measurements. These are engineering safeguards, not a complete clinical safety system. In real use, missing data would still require established clinical monitoring and an operational response outside this prototype.

## 11:00 - 12:00 | A review draft assembled from evidence

The review and handover view assembles a draft from values already available in the application. It includes the patient identifier, replay time, latest cuff measurement, pulse, relative blood volume, removal observations, predicted nadir and forecast status. The model drivers and the local review history are included as well. There is no language-model call, so the draft does not invent an explanation or add an unsupported diagnosis. The reviewer can export the text and record that it was reviewed. That local marker is not an authenticated clinical signature. The browser stores at most one hundred demo review events, and the user can export or clear them. The purpose is to demonstrate how repeated transcription could be reduced while preserving an explicit human review step and an inspectable record of what the model actually showed.

## 12:00 - 13:00 | The 90% goal has an explicit denominator

The user's ambition was to reduce work by ninety percent. We translated that into a measurable and limited target: repetitive preparation of a review draft. The default calculator assumes twenty-four patients, eight review cycles each, ninety seconds of manual preparation and nine seconds of assisted preparation. Those assumptions produce two hundred and eighty-eight minutes versus twenty-eight point eight minutes per day, a ninety-percent reduction in that preparation task. This is arithmetic, not a measured outcome. Clinical judgement, bedside assessment, treatment, corrections, training and exceptions are excluded. The inputs are editable so the claim cannot hide its denominator. A proper time-and-motion study would compare equivalent tasks, randomise order, count errors and corrections, and report the distribution of completion times. We would publish a smaller benefit honestly if that is what a study found.

## 13:00 - 14:00 | Reproducible synthetic results

The reproducible engineering benchmark uses three hundred separately seeded synthetic patients. After excluding windows already estimated below the threshold, it contains one thousand one hundred and ninety-three forecast windows. The personalised twin has a mean absolute error of about two point two five millimetres of mercury for the future pressure nadir. The recent linear-trend baseline is about two point five eight, and carrying forward the last cuff is about six point four seven. These results come from the included evaluation script, and the repository includes the metrics and each window's predictions. The benchmark patients differ from the six demonstration profiles, but the benchmark was inspected during engineering development. It is not a blinded external clinical test. The simulator and the estimator also share broad fluid-balance assumptions, which makes this an engineering comparison rather than evidence of real-world accuracy.

## 14:00 - 15:00 | Show the misses and the uncertainty

A credible result must show failures as clearly as successes. In this benchmark the twin detects one hundred and eleven of one hundred and twenty-eight future low-pressure windows and misses seventeen. It produces two false-positive windows under the selected central-trajectory threshold. The recent-trend baseline detects one hundred and six events, with seven false positives. These figures are window-level measurements; several windows can belong to the same patient, so they are correlated and must not be treated as independent clinical trials. The uncertainty band has very high empirical coverage on this generator, which can simply mean that the heuristic is broad. It is not proof of calibration. We have no real-patient external validation, no prospective alarm-burden measurement, and no basis for claiming equal performance across demographic or clinical subgroups.

## 15:00 - 16:00 | Local computation and inspectable source

The prototype runs from a small static web application. Starting it locally requires Node.js, but it does not require a package install, API key or paid inference account. A local server delivers the assets and the browser performs the calculations. Importing a synthetic session reads the file in memory and checks the supported fields and bounds. It does not upload the file. Local storage is used only for the synthetic review log. Optional web fonts affect appearance, and system fonts are used if they are unavailable. This does not make the application a secure clinical platform: authentication, encrypted clinical storage, operational monitoring and tamper-evident audit are future requirements. The value of this implementation choice is that judges can inspect the full model and reproduce the demonstration without relying on a hidden service or unavailable credentials.

## 16:00 - 17:00 | A clinical pilot must earn the next claim

The next step is evidence, not an unsupported release into patient care. First, a nephrology collaborator should help define the event, workflow and acceptable alarm burden. Retrospective data would require appropriate ethics and data-governance arrangements. Physiological priors would need to be estimated from earlier sessions without leaking future information. Evaluation should separate patients and include an external site, compare strong baselines, quantify uncertainty and report lead time, missed events and false alerts per treatment. A silent prospective study would then assess the model without allowing its predictions to influence care. Only after clinical and regulatory review should an assisted workflow be considered. The documentation includes stop or redesign criteria such as poor external generalisation, excessive alarms, over-reliance or no time benefit after corrections. None of these clinical pilot activities has been completed by this prototype.

## 17:00 - 18:00 | A funding case built around milestones

A convincing funding narrative should explain what the next investment would establish. Our initial product hypothesis is a read-only review layer for dialysis providers, used by nurses and nephrologists. It could eventually combine patient-specific forecasts, scenario comparison and documentation preparation with existing observation systems. Integration cost, pricing, procurement and support requirements remain discovery questions. We do not claim paying customers, market traction, funding or proven cost savings. The proposed milestones are clinician discovery, governed access to retrospective data, independent validation, silent prospective assessment and a measured workflow study. Existing research already addresses prediction, so differentiation must be demonstrated through useful workflow integration and transparent behaviour, not a first-ever claim. The strongest near-term asset would be an independently evaluated pilot with a clear account of both benefits and failures.

## 18:00 - 19:00 | Reproduce the prototype in three steps

To reproduce the demonstration, open the repository folder and run npm start. The server prints a local address. Opening that address displays the six synthetic sessions. Compare Meera with Arjun, move the replay slider, and inspect how the selected patient's pressure projection changes. Open the scenario lab, change the hypothetical removal rate, and inspect both pressure and fluid removed. Match the observed rate to confirm identical projections. Test dropout and restore sensors. Then open review and handover, record a review and export the draft. The evidence page provides the model explanation, downloadable sample data and evaluation output. Running npm test verifies the model's important invariants. Running npm run evaluate regenerates the documented results. The repository also contains source for the PDF documents and the narration, so the submission remains maintainable after this demonstration.

NEPHRA ends with a concrete prototype rather than a promise of a complete clinical product. It demonstrates historical and dynamic data fusion, a continuously updated patient state, a thirty-minute adverse-outcome forecast, isolated scenario exploration, quality-based withholding and an automatically assembled review draft.
